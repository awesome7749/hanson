## Heat pumps and air conditioners explained

An air conditioner moves heat from inside the home to outdoors. An air-source heat pump can reverse that process to provide heating as well as cooling. It uses electricity to run the compressor and fans while moving heat between indoors and outdoors; this is different from making all the heat with an electric resistance element.

Ducted systems distribute conditioned air through ducts. Ductless systems deliver it through indoor units serving individual rooms or zones. Either approach still needs an appropriate equipment selection and a way for conditioned air to reach the spaces you want to make comfortable. A heat pump is not limited to a wall-mounted mini-split.

Hanson Home’s recommendation is to choose the layout around the home: existing ducts and return paths, available wall or ceiling space, room use, appearance and the heating system you want to keep or replace. We can discuss equipment across brands once those requirements are clear.

## Hanson Home recommendations for common Massachusetts projects

Here is how we recommend narrowing the choices before comparing brands. These are design recommendations for common situations; the final equipment selection needs the home’s load calculation and the proposed system’s performance data.

For primary winter heating, put heating capacity at the local design temperature ahead of the highest advertised cooling efficiency. A model with strong summer ratings can still need substantial backup heat. We recommend comparing a higher winter-performance option with a clearly planned backup arrangement, including the installed price and likely operating tradeoffs.

For a house with existing ducts, first establish whether the ducts can deliver the required airflow to the rooms that need it. If the distribution works, a compatible ducted heat pump can keep the interior familiar. If one floor or bedroom has poor airflow, include the duct correction or a separate zone in the comparison. Replacing the outdoor equipment alone may leave the original comfort problem.

For an addition, upstairs rooms or a home without ducts, compare the smallest useful single-zone, multi-zone and compact ducted layouts. We recommend checking minimum output and closed-door room coverage before deciding how many indoor units to buy. One large outdoor unit is not automatically the simplest solution to several small loads.

When budget is the priority, compare complete installed proposals and ask what a higher price actually buys. Our recommendation is to preserve appropriate sizing, drainage, electrical work and commissioning, then weigh equipment upgrades against their documented comfort and energy benefits. The operating-cost examples later in this guide show why an efficiency premium may or may not pay back.

## Who makes the equipment? Four relationships to understand

Brand ownership identifies the company controlling a name. An OEM manufactures equipment; a private brand marketer sells equipment under its own label. A joint venture combines defined businesses or capabilities. A component supplier might provide the compressor or fan without designing, assembling or warranting the complete system. Those relationships should not be collapsed into one claim that two brands are “the same.”

AHRI’s certification program accepts both OEMs and private brand marketers. Its matched-system records are useful for confirming a proposed combination, but a certificate is a performance record—not a complete map of factory ownership, firmware, service support or interchangeable parts.

Our practical interpretation: shared manufacturing can create good value, yet the purchase still includes the seller’s controls, distribution and warranty arrangements. Ask for written model-specific evidence when a salesperson claims two units are identical. Matching cabinet shapes, a common compressor brand or an online discussion is insufficient evidence of identical performance or replacement-part compatibility.

> Compare what you will receive and who will support it. A manufacturing relationship is context, not a substitute for a model number.

Sources: [AHRI: how and why equipment is certified](https://www.ahrinet.org/certification/cee-directory/how-and-why-certified)

## Midea: an established manufacturer with a less visible supplier role

Midea dates its corporate roots to 1968 and its entry into air conditioning to 1985. For a Midea quote, distinguish a room air conditioner from a professionally installed ducted or ductless system. Request the US product family, matched-system certificate, control package and local parts path. Our buying interpretation is that a direct Midea-branded offer is worth evaluating on its documents and installed scope; the supplier history alone cannot establish a reliability advantage or a particular price saving.

## Bosch today: inspect the IDS generation and the expanded brand family

Bosch’s HVAC business changed substantially when it completed the Johnson Controls residential/light commercial HVAC and Johnson Controls–Hitachi Air Conditioning transactions on July 31, 2025. Its release describes long-term licenses for YORK and Hitachi branding. That is a corporate portfolio change; it does not make a YORK system interchangeable with an existing Bosch IDS system.

On its IDS Ultra page, Bosch describes enhanced vapor injection and publishes up-to figures of 19 SEER2, 10 HSPF2 and 100% heating capacity at 5°F with COP 2.1. The page also distinguishes continued heating at lower temperatures. These are manufacturer claims for the Ultra family. They should not be applied to IDS Light or older IDS models.

Our buying interpretation: an IDS offer can be attractive for a ducted retrofit, but the family suffix matters. Compare its cold-weather output, approved air handler, duct airflow and thermostat instructions. An attractive price on a different IDS generation is not an equivalent substitute for a quoted cold-climate combination.

> Hanson Home recommendation: for a ducted winter-heating project, compare the exact IDS generation with the existing ducts, indoor unit and backup plan. Consider IDS Ultra where its verified winter performance adds value; do not pay for or assume Ultra performance on a different IDS tier.

## Sanyo and Panasonic: a legacy name with a real HVAC lineage

Panasonic’s HVAC history includes Sanyo’s 1947 roots, alongside Panasonic’s own AC business launch in 1957 and first room cooler in 1958. Panasonic made Sanyo wholly owned in April 2011. A Panasonic-issued September 2011 release then announced that Sanyo’s North American HVAC group would be incorporated into Panasonic’s operations effective October 1, 2011.

For an owner of an older Sanyo mini-split, the relevant question is the service path for that particular installed system. Before approving a repair, ask whether its control board, sensors, fan motor and refrigerant-side components remain available, and compare the complete repair price with a properly designed replacement.

For a new purchase, ask which current US Panasonic model and distributor are actually being offered. Our interpretation: Sanyo is useful historical context and a legacy service issue, not a reason to assume a modern Panasonic indoor unit will connect to an old Sanyo outdoor unit. Require a manufacturer-approved match and current warranty terms.

> Hanson Home recommendation: with an older Sanyo installation, establish the available repair parts and complete repair cost before choosing a replacement. If replacement is appropriate, revisit the room loads and layout rather than automatically copying the old unit’s nominal size.

## Inside one brand: Daikin’s own tiers show why the model matters

Daikin’s May 2026 R-32 comparison sheet lists DH9VS with up to 21 SEER2 and 10 HSPF2, compared with DH7VS at up to 19 SEER2 and 8.8 HSPF2. Both are listed with variable-speed swing compressors and communicating controls; the sheet identifies DH9VS, but not DH7VS, as meeting the DOE Cold Climate Heat Pump Challenge.

The same sheet distinguishes variable-speed models from two-stage and other scroll-compressor models. These are design and product-tier differences inside one manufacturer’s lineup. The DOE Challenge is a particular performance program; its presence or absence does not, by itself, size the system for your house.

Our interpretation: a lower-cost model from a respected brand may be a sensible cooling or supplemental-heating choice, while a primary-heating project can justify a different tier. Ask for the selected combination’s low-temperature tables. Neither a corporate reputation nor an inverter label establishes equal winter capability across all tiers.

> Hanson Home recommendation: ask what changes between the quoted Daikin tiers in your actual application. For a primary-heating project, winter capacity and the complete control package can justify a different choice than for occasional cooling or supplemental heat.

## How Hanson Home would turn the TCL data into an installation decision

Consider an illustrative house that needs 32,000 BTU/h at 5°F. If the quoted system delivers 26,000 BTU/h at the same condition, the simple capacity difference is 6,000 BTU/h. ## SEER2, EER2, HSPF2 and COP answer different questions

SEER2 describes seasonal cooling efficiency; EER2 describes cooling efficiency at a specified rating condition; HSPF2 describes seasonal heating efficiency. DOE’s 2023 standards documentation uses the revised “2” metrics. Compare the same metric and rating basis. A brochure using older SEER/HSPF should not be placed in a numeric ranking against SEER2/HSPF2 without an appropriate conversion and equipment context.

COP, or coefficient of performance, compares delivered heat with electrical energy input at a given condition. Both quantities must use consistent units. A COP of 2 means two units of heat delivered per unit of electricity consumed. COP is useful for a cold-weather point; it is not an annual bill forecast.

When reviewing specifications, ask whether a capacity/COP point is rated, minimum-speed or maximum-speed operation. NEEP’s sizing tools compare equipment output and building loads. A high seasonal score and a strong maximum-capacity point can coexist with different performance at the temperatures and speeds your home actually uses.

Our buying approach: keep two columns in your comparison—seasonal metrics and winter design-point data. A cooling-focused buyer may give SEER2 more weight; a household relying on the equipment for winter heating needs the low-temperature capacity, input power and backup plan alongside HSPF2.

Sources: [DOE: 2023 central AC and heat-pump standards FAQ](https://www1.eere.energy.gov/buildings/appliance_standards/pdfs/2023_CAC_Standards_FAQ_10-5-2022_Final.pdf); [NEEP: cold-climate sizing support tools](https://neep.org/blog/not-too-big-not-too-small-new-tools-improved-air-source-heat-pump-selection)

## Cold-weather capacity, vapor injection and the balance point

As outdoor conditions change, the home’s heating demand and the heat pump’s available output change. NEEP’s selection guidance calls for manufacturer performance near the outdoor design condition. The temperature at which available heat matches the home’s load is often called the thermal balance point; below it, a capacity shortfall must be addressed by the system design.

Bosch describes enhanced vapor injection in IDS Ultra; METUS describes flash injection in its 2026 SMART MULTI announcement. These approaches support low-temperature compressor performance. Their presence is not enough to size equipment: the final output and input-power tables remain the evidence that matters.

Our interpretation for Massachusetts buyers: “heats down to −22°F” and “delivers the calculated heating load at −22°F” are separate promises. Ask for the documented outdoor design temperature for your location, the indoor design temperature, load at that condition and available delivered output. Coastal and inland locations should not automatically receive the same weather assumption.

Also ask how the design accounts for defrost and backup operation. A family’s advertised lowest operating temperature should not be treated as proof of complete-house coverage, zero auxiliary heat or low operating cost.

## Minimum output and zoning: the specification buyers often miss

An inverter compressor can vary output, but it still has a minimum stable capacity. NEEP identifies minimum capacity as an important selection criterion and warns against choosing multi-zone equipment only by the desired number of rooms. Oversizing can leave too much output available when the home needs little heat or cooling.

Our illustrative example: a small bedroom needs 2,000 BTU/h on a mild day, while a proposed outdoor system cannot operate steadily below 8,000 BTU/h under that condition. A large maximum capacity does not solve the mismatch. Ask how the design handles a call from that bedroom alone and how other indoor units behave.

Compare three layouts before assuming one large outdoor unit is best: several single-zone systems; a smaller multi-zone system; and a compact ducted unit serving several small rooms. Evaluate outdoor space, piping, indoor appearance, minimum output, service access and what remains available during a repair.

For summer comfort, ask about minimum cooling output and humidity control as well. A system that reaches the thermostat setting quickly is not necessarily providing the most comfortable moisture removal. A room-by-room heat-load and cooling-load review makes the discussion much more concrete.

## Ducts and controls can change the result across brands

For a ducted retrofit, ask the contractor to evaluate airflow and external static pressure—the resistance the blower works against. A new outdoor unit cannot correct inadequate return paths or every existing duct problem. Bosch’s FAQs specifically connect comfort problems with sizing, duct layout, static pressure and control setup.

Control approaches also differ. Bosch says IDS Light does not require a communicating thermostat and works with most 24VAC thermostats. Midea’s EVOX G3 page describes both 24V and 485 compatibility; TCL’s cited Advantage combination lists 24VAC and TCL RS485 control options. Daikin’s comparison sheet identifies communicating models. These examples are model-specific, not universal rules for their brands.

Our comparison questions: Will the existing thermostat work? Will retaining it change available functions? Which controller regulates indoor fan speed and auxiliary heat? Can the homeowner operate the system if Wi-Fi is unavailable? Is remote temperature sensing included? What control and sensor replacements would cost should be part of the discussion.

Have the quote name the exact thermostat, interfaces and backup control sequence. For a multi-zone layout, ask what happens when one room wants heating while another wants cooling; do not assume independent temperature settings mean unrestricted simultaneous modes.

> Hanson Home recommendation: include duct corrections, thermostat interfaces and backup controls in the quote comparison. A lower equipment price can lose its advantage if those necessary pieces are missing from the scope.

## R-32 versus R-454B: what a consumer should compare

EPA lists R-32 and R-454B as A2L refrigerants subject to use conditions. On EPA’s Technology Transitions reference basis, their global warming potentials are 675 and 465 respectively, compared with 2,088 for R-410A. Keep the reference basis consistent: another document may quote slightly different values based on a different assessment.

A2L is a safety classification, not an efficiency score. Equipment and installation must be designed for the specified refrigerant. A lower GWP does not automatically produce a higher COP, quieter unit or lower bill; those depend on the complete system and operating conditions.

Our buying approach: ask what refrigerant the quoted equipment uses, which installation safeguards the manual requires, and whether the contractor is trained and equipped to service it. Compare service availability and approved components alongside environmental impact. Do not assume a different refrigerant can be added to an existing system as a simple upgrade.

Sources: [EPA SNAP: residential AC / heat-pump refrigerant alternatives](https://www.epa.gov/snap/substitutes-residential-and-light-commercial-air-conditioning-and-heat-pumps); [EPA: Technology Transitions GWP reference table](https://www.epa.gov/hfcs/technology-transitions-gwp-reference-table)

## Operating cost: two calculations more useful than a brand slogan

The following examples use an assumed electricity price of $0.30/kWh. It is not a current Massachusetts tariff. Use your own bill’s applicable electricity charges and recognize that fixed charges may not change with heating use. These are simplified arithmetic examples, not seasonal savings estimates.

Heating example: suppose the system must deliver 30,000 BTU/h. Using 3,412 BTU per kWh, that is about 8.79 kW of delivered heat. At COP 3, electrical input is about 2.93 kW, costing $0.88 per hour. At COP 2, input is about 4.40 kW, costing $1.32 per hour. At COP 1, resistance heating delivering the same heat costs about $2.64 per hour. Runtime, changing loads, defrost and auxiliaries will alter real totals.

Cooling example: under identical seasonal load and rating assumptions, changing from 16 SEER2 to 20 SEER2 implies roughly 16 ÷ 20 = 0.80 of the cooling electricity, or a 20% reduction in that simplified comparison. If the annual cooling electricity would otherwise cost $500, the illustration is about $100 per year. It is not a prediction of a specific home’s bill.

Our buying interpretation: compare the price premium with realistic heating and cooling savings, maintenance and service terms. If a more efficient option costs $2,000 extra and plausibly saves $100 annually, a simple energy-only payback would be 20 years. Comfort improvements, financing, maintenance and future rates may change the decision, but should be stated separately.

> Hanson Home recommendation: ask us to compare the equipment premium with the comfort benefit and a realistic operating-cost scenario using your rates. Spending more can be worthwhile, but the explanation should identify which improvement matters in your house and what assumptions support the savings.
